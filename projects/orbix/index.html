ORBIX

ORBIX is an aerospace engineering web app built around real aircraft and launch vehicles. You can drag an orbit and watch the delta-v change, plan a mission burn by burn, or put three aircraft side by side. Its results are checked against published worked examples. The idea, the research, and every decision are mine. AI coding assistants wrote the code under my direction.

What it is

I plan to study aerospace engineering, and I wanted one place where I could look at real vehicles and work through the engineering behind them: lift and drag, the rocket equation, orbital transfers, shock waves, and entry heating. ORBIX is that place, and it is free for anyone to use.

The home page opens on the Transfer Explorer. Drag the target orbit anywhere from 160 km to 400,000 km and ORBIX recomputes the two burns of a Hohmann transfer, the total delta-v, and the coast time, then explains each burn in a sentence.

How I built it

My part, and what the AI tools did.

ORBIX was my idea from the start. I chose which aircraft and launch vehicles to include, which calculators and mission scenarios to build, and how the pieces connect. I researched the vehicle specifications and the engineering behind every tool, then wrote instructions with the exact equations, inputs, and units each part needed. I also set the standards the site had to meet for design, accessibility, and the legal side of running a public website, and I reviewed the results against them.

I am an aspiring aerospace engineer, not a software engineer, so I used AI coding assistants to write the software: Codex by OpenAI for the first version, then Claude Code by Anthropic. Working from my instructions, they wrote the code, the automated tests, and the page layouts. The full history is public on GitHub, and the commits made with Claude Code are marked as co-authored by it.

I built it this way on purpose. Engineers in every field are going to work alongside these tools, and I wanted to learn to use them well: describe exactly what I want, check what comes back, and refuse to accept something until it is right.

The Engineering Lab

13 calculators and a mission planner.

Every tool in the lab shows its equation, its units, and its assumptions next to the result. The calculators fall into three groups:

Foundations
Rocket equation, thrust-to-weight, lift, drag, standard atmosphere, flight condition
Compressible flow
Stagnation condition, normal shock, oblique shock, supersonic inlet compression, stagnation-point heating
Orbits and missions
Hohmann transfer, orbital plane change, mission planner

The mission planner strings those calculations together. Pick a preset such as a LEO satellite deployment or a lunar transfer, and it lists every burn in flight order with its delta-v. Steps it does not model, like the launch itself, show up as plain rows that say so. The presets then appear on one shared axis, so a lunar transfer at 4,049 m/s sits next to a 199.5 m/s orbit raise at the same scale.

Checking the math

26 of 36 values within rounding.

To check the calculators, ORBIX has a Verification page. It runs selected lab functions with the inputs from published tables and worked examples, then prints each result beside the published value. The ORBIX numbers come from the same functions the lab uses. Nobody types them in.

The sources are Robert A. Braeunig's example problems, Brennen's Internet Book on Fluid Dynamics, NACA Report 1135, and the U.S. Standard Atmosphere, 1976. Of 36 compared values, 26 fall within the rounding of the published figure. The other 10 stay on the page with a reason for each. In the Hohmann case below, the source rounds each speed before subtracting, while ORBIX keeps full precision, so the two disagree by about 1 m/s.

Real vehicles

Five aircraft and five launch vehicles.

The aircraft are the F-22, F-35, F-15, B-2, and SR-71. The launch vehicles are Falcon 9, Falcon Heavy, SLS, Saturn V, and Starship. Each one has a profile with its published specifications, and every photo has a verified license and a credit.

Public sources often disagree, so ORBIX shows values in the units the source used and never converts them quietly. A missing value reads "Not published" instead of zero, and the compare page gives no ranking and no winner. The scale drawings are outlines traced from public-domain government drawings and sized to each vehicle's recorded height or length.

How it is organized

The physics is kept separate from the pages. Each calculator is a small function for one calculation, analyses combine several calculators into one study, and the React components only draw the results. That separation is what lets the Verification page call the exact functions the lab uses.

Every change runs through GitHub Actions before it reaches the live site: formatting, linting, type checks, 1,280 unit tests, a production build, and a separate browser test suite at desktop, tablet, and phone sizes, which includes accessibility checks.

What I learned

Building ORBIX taught me the basics of every aircraft and launch vehicle in it: what each one was designed to do and how its design serves that purpose.

The bigger lesson came from the Engineering Lab. Before any tool could be built, I had to research how it works: which equation it uses, what goes into it, and what the result means. Across every tool in the lab, that meant learning how lift, drag, thrust, gravity, shock waves, and heating actually behave, and working out a way for each one to be calculated.

I also learned what it is like to run simulations: change one input, run it again, and see how an orbital transfer or a shock wave responds. And I got real experience with how software gets made, from planning features to testing and fixing mistakes.
